Why Beyond Engineering?

Engineering as the Technical Foundation

My engineering portfolio demonstrates capability in flight dynamics, autonomous systems, structural analysis, aircraft systems design, CAD, simulation and systems integration. These projects show that I can move from an operational problem to a technical architecture, develop a model or design, evaluate its performance and communicate the engineering meaning of the results.

Purpose Beyond the Technical Problem

Engineering does not operate in isolation. Aircraft, defence systems, launch vehicles and national infrastructure depend upon research institutions, manufacturing capability, public investment, strategic priorities, legal frameworks and responsible decision-making. Studying those wider systems helps me understand not only how technology is built, but why it is built, how it is governed and how it contributes to national capability and society.

Engineering with Purpose

The technical projects in my portfolio are linked by a recurring interest in aerospace, defence, autonomy and the development of indigenous engineering capability.

Aerospace and Flight Systems

Aircraft combine aerodynamics, structures, propulsion, control, electronics and systems integration within one safety-critical platform. My work in flight-control optimisation, flight dynamics, aircraft CAD and autonomous UAV development reflects my interest in designing and improving these multidisciplinary systems.

Portfolio evidence: Cessna 182 flight-control optimisation, aircraft CAD and drafting, and the autonomous UAV behavioural flight-control architecture.

Defence Systems and National Capability

Defence engineering requires reliability, systems thinking, mission awareness and the integration of multiple technologies under operational constraints. My interest in indigenous defence capability is reflected in projects involving aircraft modernisation, structural redesign and autonomous aerial systems.

Portfolio evidence: MiG-21 Bison modernisation, PC-9 wing redesign and autonomous UAV development.

Space, Autonomy and Scientific Progress

Space systems represent the integration of propulsion, guidance, structures, communications, autonomy and scientific research. My interest in space and autonomous systems comes from their potential to support navigation, communications, Earth observation, disaster response, research and long-term technological capability.

Technical direction: MATLAB/Simulink modelling, autonomous control, systems engineering and future work in reusable launch-vehicle guidance and recovery.

Infrastructure and National Development

Engineering capability becomes nationally significant when it extends beyond individual products into transport, manufacturing, energy, research, communications and industrial infrastructure. The inclusion of ISRO, modern cities and Vande Bharat in the visual represents my interest in how technological systems collectively contribute to national development.

Systems perspective: technology, institutions, manufacturing, skills and infrastructure must develop together for engineering progress to create lasting impact.

Why Governance, Behaviour and Society Matter to an Engineer

Constitution and Institutions

Engineering programmes are shaped by institutions that establish priorities, allocate resources, regulate safety and remain accountable to the public. My interest in constitutional studies and governance helps me examine how those institutions operate, where responsibility is located and how decisions affect society.

Human Behaviour and Security

Technology is designed, operated and sometimes misused by people. Behaviour, group influence, incentives and social conditions can therefore affect the performance and consequences of technical systems. Criminal psychology provided a framework for examining those human factors, particularly in relation to radicalisation, organised violence and security.

Human Rights and Accountability

Engineering in defence, autonomy, surveillance and public infrastructure requires more than technical success. It also requires legal boundaries, institutional accountability and awareness of how decisions affect individuals and communities. Human-rights study strengthened my ability to evaluate competing claims, institutional responsibility and the difference between stated principles and their practical enforcement.

Community Building and National Development

Technology contributes to nation-building when it improves capability, resilience, access, security and opportunity. I am interested in how engineering can support that process while remaining connected to responsible governance, social cohesion and long-term public value.

Case File 001

Human Rights, Universality and Political Selectivity

Completed
Central Question: Are human rights genuinely universal, or are modern human-rights systems shaped by Western political history and selective institutional enforcement?

This research examined the distinction between the universal moral aspiration of human rights and the political reality of how rights are interpreted, prioritised and enforced. The paper considered the historical development of modern human-rights frameworks while assessing whether institutions apply those principles consistently across different communities and political contexts.

The analysis drew upon historical and contemporary case studies, including Noakhali, the exodus of Kashmiri Hindus and the 26/11 Mumbai attacks, to examine institutional accountability, political attention and the limits of universal enforcement.

Case File 002

Criminal Psychology and Radicalisation

Completed
Central Question: How can behavioural and criminological theory help explain the process through which an individual becomes involved in organised terrorism?

This research applied criminal-psychology and criminological theories to the case of Ajmal Kasab and the 26/11 Mumbai attacks. The objective was not simply to describe the event, but to investigate the behavioural mechanisms that may contribute to recruitment, indoctrination, radicalisation and participation in organised violence.

The study applied Social Learning Theory, Strain Theory and Social Control Theory to examine how environment, grievance, group influence, weakened social bonds and exposure to violent norms may interact within the radicalisation process.
